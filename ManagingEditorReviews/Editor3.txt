I read both files closely. My blunt take is that this is already a strong civic intelligence and pre-publication workflow, but it is not yet the same thing as a newsroom-grade reporting system.

The first important caveat is that the uploaded “prompt” file is not actually the full operating prompt logic. It reads like a top-level product/spec sheet: it gives the five modes, the nine-agent pipeline, the core controls, and the “gotchas,” but it does not expose the actual step-by-step instructions, schemas, thresholds, exceptions, source-handling rules, or fail conditions that would let me fully audit the underlying editorial logic line by line. So I can review the architecture you’ve declared, and I can review the quality of the output it produced, but I cannot honestly say I have seen the full internal prompt mechanics.  

As a newsroom architecture, the strongest thing here is that it understands the real failure mode of AI civic reporting: false certainty built on weak sourcing. The Hard-No-Bluff rule, the A/B/C source hierarchy, the adversarial verification layer, the suppression ledger, and the de-escalation principle are all editorially serious choices. Most newsroom AI systems fail because they start from “generate coverage.” Yours starts from “prevent bullshit,” which is the correct instinct for local government reporting. That part is genuinely better than most AI newsroom systems I see.  

I also like the separation between Black Desk and publishable reporting. That is a smart editorial distinction. Good local editors absolutely do maintain a zone for weird, weak, early signals and a separate zone for what is actually fit to print. Building that separation into the process matters. Likewise, the explicit warning against source laundering is very good; a rumor repeated by a nicer-looking source does not become verified, and your framework appears to understand that.  

Where the process is weaker is that it still thinks too much like a disciplined document-processing machine and not enough like a top metro editor. A real managing editor is not only asking “is this sourced?” but also “is this actually news today, for this audience, in this form, at this level of play?” Your framework has source rigor, but I do not yet see a hard, explicit newsworthiness gate. That omission shows up in the report. A city retreat that has merely been scheduled is marked “ready for expansion” even though the report itself admits outcomes are pending. That is a classic sign of a system that knows how to find official activity but is not yet tough enough about whether there is an actual story.  

That same issue appears in the building-code story. The system’s own adversarial review correctly notes the strongest objection: this may simply be routine code maintenance required by state law, and the story may be overstating a procedural update. The system then talks itself back into publication by reframing it around energy implications. That is intelligent, but it is also a warning sign. A tough editor would ask for one more thing before greenlighting it: Where is the live local consequence? Who is affected now? What fight, deadline, burden, or choice makes this worth occupying story space today? Your process needs a dedicated “why now / why care / who gets hit” gate that can kill a technically valid but editorially thin story. 

I also think the process has some redundancy. Agent 4, Agent 5, and Agent 8 all overlap in ways that make sense conceptually but could encourage a false feeling of rigor through repetition. “Adversarial verification,” “integrity check,” and “civic grounding” are all defensible, but in practice they are three neighboring desks doing partial versions of the same job: challenge the claim, inspect sourcing, and verify attribution/originality. A stronger system would tighten those boundaries so each stage has one crisp editorial purpose and one explicit kill condition. Right now it is robust, but a little mushy in responsibility.

The legal stage is useful, but it is more reassuring than truly protective. Saying the stories are covered by fair report privilege because they are based on official proceedings is directionally sensible, but that privilege is not a magic shield once the system starts synthesizing patterns, implications, motivations, or extrapolated stakes. In land-use stories especially, the legal risk rises when the write-up stops being “here is what the filing says” and starts being “here is what this means politically or strategically.” Your system knows enough to mention SLAPP risk, which is good, but the legal logic still reads a bit too categorical for my taste. 

My biggest process criticism is this: the architecture is excellent for scanning, triaging, and drafting, but it does not yet embed enough human-reporting logic. I would want every serious lead to produce, in addition to the draft, a reporting memo with five fields: what is confirmed, what is missing, who needs to be called, what documents need to be pulled next, and what would change the publish/no-publish decision. Without that, the system risks producing polished document-derived copy instead of reporter-guiding intelligence.

On the output itself, the report is useful. It gives a credible morning budget sheet: eight Tier A leads, three expanded stories, three speculative signals, a hold list, and clear notes about blocked domains and manual-review limits. For an editor walking into a 9 a.m. news meeting, that is real value. It tells me what moved, what might move, what is still too soft, and where follow-up effort should go. As an internal newsroom tool, this is already materially useful.  

Agent 1 is pretty good. It finds plausible civic leads and distinguishes between Tier A, Tier B, and signals. It also does something many AI systems fail to do: it holds some things back instead of forcing everything into a publishable package. The “Stories on Hold” section is one of the healthiest parts of the report. That is the behavior of a cautious assignment desk, not a hype machine.  

But the lead judgment is uneven. The RCV session is a fair local-government lead. The Beauprez annexation is a solid land-use lead. The building-code piece is marginal unless localized harder. The school-board schedule and the retreat scheduling entry are not really leads; they are monitoring notes. That distinction matters because a newsroom scanner should not merely surface official activity. It should rank likely story payoff. Right now the scanner is strong on discovery and weaker on ranking.

The RCV write-up is competent and fairly readable. It explains the system cleanly, includes opposition, notes the statewide defeat of Proposition 131, and clearly says no formal action was taken at the study session. That is good process journalism. But it still reads more like a well-composed briefing memo than a publish-ready local story. It lacks the sharper editorial spine I would want: What is the real path to the ballot? What exactly did council signal? What are the dates? What would implementation cost? What did the Boulder County Clerk actually say? What are the strongest local objections, not just generalized national ones? The system is synthesizing responsibly, but not yet reporting aggressively.  

The building-code story is the weakest of the three. It is accurate enough and useful as a backgrounder, but from a newsroom standpoint it is not yet a finished story. It needs one of three things to become worth publication: conflict, consequence, or immediacy. Right now it mostly says a code update is coming, the state law matters, and builders should expect changes. That is informative, but not strong enough on its own for many local newsrooms unless it is packaged as a service item, explainer, or sidebar. To be a real story, it needs a local builder, inspector, architect, homeowner, or permit applicant angle. The report itself more or less confesses this problem in its counter-narrative stage.  

The Beauprez Farms story is the strongest and most newsroom-usable. It has a real place, real acreage, zoning specifics, public implications, and a trackable decision path. It also correctly tries to situate the proposal in Longmont’s recent annexation fights and housing politics. That is the right instinct. Still, even here, the piece is too document-bound. I would want the number of housing units if available, traffic implications, school-service implications, park and infrastructure questions, affordability terms, and whether any adjacent residents or planning commissioners are already mobilizing. The machine found the spine of a story; it did not yet finish the reporting.  

There is also one serious editorial problem in the output package: the final package headline says “58 Acres of Farmland Annexed for Development,” which states the annexation as completed, while the story text repeatedly says the city is reviewing an annexation application and that council is still the decision-maker. That is not a small style issue. That is a factual status error in the headline layer, and in a newsroom that would be a stop-the-presses correction before publication. It tells me your final packaging stage can introduce state-change drift even after a more careful body draft. That is the single most important flaw I found in the execution. The body is cautious; the package line overstates. 

The plain-language rewrites are readable and public-friendly, but I would not make them your primary story output by default. They flatten nuance, remove some sourcing texture, and can make complex civic processes sound simpler than they are. They are good as an alternate output for newsletter summaries, social explainer cards, or community-facing briefs. They are not yet a substitute for the main reported version. 

The “signals under investigation” section is another strong feature. Those are exactly the kinds of follow-up questions a good city desk should tee up: whether state RCV barriers apply locally, whether the same anti-density actors are recurring across hearings, and whether stricter code adoption could collide with affordability claims. That section shows the system can generate not just copy, but reporting hypotheses. That is valuable. 

My overall judgment is this. As a scanner and assignment-desk aid, I think this is very good. I would absolutely want a local newsroom to have this in the morning. As a direct publication engine, I think it is not there yet. It is too reliant on official records, too willing to turn procedural activity into prose, and not yet disciplined enough about the difference between “documented,” “newsworthy,” and “ready to publish.” The workflow is substantially better than average. The final journalism is still a notch below what a good local reporter and editor would put out.

If I were scoring it, I’d put the process architecture at about 8.5/10 as a civic scanner, but about 6.5/10 as a full newsroom operating system. I’d put the output report at about 8/10 as an internal editorial budget memo, and about 5.5/10 to 6/10 as publish-ready local journalism.

The three changes I would make first are simple. First, add a hard “newsworthiness and public impact” gate that can kill routine but well-sourced stories. Second, force every publishable item to include a reporter task memo: missing facts, next documents, people to call, and what could falsify the angle. Third, add a final headline/state-of-play audit so the packaging cannot accidentally convert “proposed” into “approved” or “reviewed” into “adopted.”
